Optimizers and Their Differences: SGD, Momentum, Nesterov, RMSProp, AdamW, Prodigy

ML notes for admitted.dk

Everyone meets these optimizers as a dropdown in a training script: SGD, Momentum, Nesterov, RMSProp, AdamW, Prodigy. The names are famous and the differences are usually explained in words, or in a static diagram of update arrows. Words are cheap and arrows lie. So this page races them instead: six optimizers, dropped from the same point on an actual loss surface, each running its real update rule in your browser until it stops.

Two surfaces. First a clean tilted bowl, where the only question is how each optimizer handles curvature. Then a rougher one with several wells of different depths, where the question becomes which hole each one actually falls into, and whether that is the deepest one. Click anywhere to drop the field.

Plain gradient descent (black) zigzags hardest when the bowl is most stretched out, at low regularization, and travels straighter once the slider rounds it off, though push the strength far enough and even a round bowl gets steep enough to make a fixed step size wobble again. The adaptive methods barely react either way, they rescale their own steps to whatever curvature they find underfoot. It also just needs more steps to get anywhere, which is why it's still moving after the others have stopped. Dupont's original gave plain SGD a bigger learning rate than everyone else too (2e-2 against 1e-2, in his own words "to make it converge faster"), and it still took 500 steps against Adam's 100. Same idea here: SGD and Prodigy both run longer than the rest.

These races run at a fixed 9 milliseconds per step, the same timing for all six optimizers. SGD and Prodigy take 400 steps each, Momentum and Nesterov take 200, and RMSProp and AdamW take 150. Watch closely and some paths will freeze mid-slide while others keep rolling: when a path stops, that optimizer has finished its steps and you're seeing the literal difference in iteration count. That frozen moment while other optimizers continue is the step-budget inequality made visible.

AdamW is worth watching closely here, and it's the one that can look broken at first glance: at higher regularization its path stops well short of the ring center, marked instead with a small green diamond of its own. That's not it giving up early. The other five fold the regularization straight into the gradient, the same mechanism as ridge in Fig. 1 and 2, and all converge on the same ring center. AdamW instead subtracts the regularization from the weights directly on every step, decoupled from its adaptive part entirely. That decoupling is the actual difference between Adam and AdamW, and it's why AdamW's diamond consistently lands closer to zero than the ring the other five are aiming for.

One optimizer here has no learning rate at all. Prodigy estimates its own step size from how far it has already travelled from where it started, which is also why it looks hesitant for the first stretch of any race: it's still working out the scale of the problem.

A rougher hill

Remember the bumps and false valleys from the opening. Fig. 3's bowl didn't actually have any, one smooth slope down. Here's a surface that does: several wells of different depths, one of them the real bottom, the rest just convincing enough to trap something rolling through. Click to drop the same six optimizers in and watch where they actually end up. Different starting points, and different optimizers from the same starting point, can land in entirely different holes.

Regularization helps here too, and the slider does the same thing it did in Fig. 3: it adds a pull toward the center on top of the existing surface. Turn it up and watch the shallow pits get swallowed by that pull first, the surface simplifies down to a single basin, and it stops mattering where anyone started.

At low regularization, watch where the deepest well actually is versus where most starting points end up: the true bottom isn't always the easiest one to reach. That's a real, general fact about optimizing anything complicated, a gradient only ever tells you which way is down from where you're standing, never whether there's a better down somewhere else. Momentum can carry an optimizer straight through a shallow pit it would otherwise have stopped in, which is one reason it's rarely turned off in practice.

This figure uses a different timing model: all six paths are drawn as a fraction of their total length over one shared 4-second window, so every optimizer finishes at exactly the same moment regardless of how many internal steps it took. Prodigy needs 6,000 steps to warm up on this flatter surface, compared to 400 for SGD, 200 for Momentum and Nesterov, and 150 each for RMSProp and AdamW. That early stillness in Prodigy's path while others keep moving is real, it's the algorithm slowly building signal before it dares to step, just time-compressed into a normal animation length. The gap in raw work, hidden by the shared duration, is why Prodigy needs more steps to get anywhere: thousands of tiny exploratory moves versus hundreds for the rest.

Loss surfaces and fitted curves above are computed live from closed-form ridge and lasso solutions, not illustrative sketches. Figs. 3 and 4's optimizer paths are real gradient descent, run in your browser.
